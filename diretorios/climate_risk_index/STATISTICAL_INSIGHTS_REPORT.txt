4. RISK STRUCTURE AND MISMATCH
================================================================================

The mismatch indicator measures how unbalanced the three main dimensions are
inside each municipality-year:

    Hazard
    Exposure
    Vulnerability

It is calculated as the standard deviation across the three subindices.

High mismatch means that one or two dimensions are relatively high while another
dimension is low. Because the final Climate Risk Index has a multiplicative
structure, this imbalance can compress the final aggregate risk score.


4.1 Full panel, 2002-2023
-------------------------

Mismatch statistics:

    Mean mismatch:             0.201
    Standard deviation:        0.083
    75th percentile threshold: 0.261
    High-mismatch share:       25.0%


4.2 Latest year, 2023
---------------------

Mismatch statistics:

    Mean mismatch:             0.182
    Standard deviation:        0.077
    75th percentile threshold: 0.244
    High-mismatch share:       25.1%


Interpretation:

    About one quarter of the municipalities are classified as high mismatch by
    construction, because the threshold is the 75th percentile.

    The substantive interpretation is that many municipalities may have latent
    climate-industrial risk. For example, a municipality can have high hazard or
    vulnerability but low industrial exposure, which reduces the final aggregate
    risk score.

    This is important for policy interpretation: a low final index does not
    always mean the absence of climate pressure. It can also mean that the three
    dimensions are not simultaneously high.

5. CORRELATION BETWEEN SUBINDICES
================================================================================

5.1 Full panel, 2002-2023
-------------------------

Correlation matrix:

    Hazard x Exposure:        -0.290
    Hazard x Vulnerability:   -0.101
    Exposure x Vulnerability: -0.197

Interpretation:

    The correlations between subindices are negative. This suggests that the
    three dimensions are not simply moving together.

    The strongest relationship is between Hazard and Exposure (-0.290). This
    means municipalities with higher industrial exposure are not necessarily the
    same municipalities with higher normalized hazard levels.

    This supports the need for a multidimensional index. Hazard alone, exposure
    alone or vulnerability alone would not capture the full structure of risk.


5.2 Latest year, 2023
---------------------

Correlation matrix:

    Hazard x Exposure:        -0.245
    Hazard x Vulnerability:   -0.018
    Exposure x Vulnerability: -0.203

Interpretation:

    In 2023, Hazard and Vulnerability are almost uncorrelated. Exposure and
    Vulnerability remain moderately negative, and Hazard and Exposure also remain
    negative.

    This reinforces the interpretation that climate pressure, industrial
    exposure and socioeconomic vulnerability are distinct dimensions. Their
    combination is analytically more informative than any single component.

================================================================================
7. MAIN ANALYTICAL TAKEAWAYS
================================================================================

1. Risk is concentrated, not evenly distributed.

    The Climate Risk Index has positive skewness in both the full panel and in
    2023. Most municipalities have lower risk, while a smaller group has
    substantially higher risk.


2. The index is multidimensional by necessity.

    Hazard, Exposure and Vulnerability are weakly to moderately negatively
    correlated. They do not measure the same thing. This supports the index
    design.


3. Mismatch matters.

    A municipality can have high pressure in one dimension and low pressure in
    another. Because the final index is multiplicative, this can reduce the final
    score even when one dimension is concerning.


4. Industrial exposure behaves consistently.

    Industrial jobs per capita and industrial firms per capita are strongly
    positively correlated, which supports the internal consistency of the
    Exposure subindex.


5. Vulnerability has an economic structure.

    Energy intensity is positively related to industrial employment, while
    agricultural sensitivity and income-resilience vulnerability tend to be lower
    in more industrial municipalities.


6. Correlations do not imply causality.

    These statistics describe association patterns in the index inputs. They do
    not prove causal relationships between climate variables, industrial
    structure and vulnerability.
